10/10 IF IT HOLDS

Coordinator’s verdict

10/10 if it holds, and it isn’t verified yet. The Lean-formalized core is strong evidence, but only one independent re-check had finished at the time of writing, and the biggest unformalized claims (BSD, Hodge for CM abelian varieties, Hilbert’s 16th, Artin, Hilbert’s 10th over ℚ) are preprints from an anonymous author until experts check them.

A second, independent pass that read only the family headings picked nearly the same top list (quasi-RH, Hilbert’s 16th, UGC, ω ≤ 9/4, Hilbert’s 10th over ℚ, free group factors, Thompson’s F, Cannon) and rated the package 9/10, docking a point because the Lean statements are marked ‘review: unchecked’ and several giants have no formal proof.

01Verdict

Package rating: 10 / 10 if correct. That rating means "changes how mathematics is done", and it does not depend on everything being right. The Lean-checked subset alone would be enough: the quasi-Riemann hypothesis at 7/8, the Unique Games Conjecture, matrix multiplication with ω ≤ 9/4, no percolation at criticality on ℤ³, non-amenability of Thompson's group F, Cannon's conjecture, isomorphism of the free group factors, spontaneous magnetization of the quantum Heisenberg ferromagnet, global smoothness for relativistic Vlasov–Maxwell and Erdős's arithmetic-progression conjecture. That would be the largest single addition to mathematics ever published at once, and it would come from a machine.

Confidence that it is correct: not yet established. Only one independent Lean check has finished so far: a CMU PhD student re-ran the Comparator on the Falconer result, and it passed. His run on the quasi-Riemann hypothesis was still going at the time of writing. The repository's own catalogue marks the formal review "unchecked", and 137 of the 372 families have no formal proof at all. Those 137 include some of the largest claims: the BSD formula, Hodge for CM abelian varieties, Artin's conjecture, Hilbert's tenth problem over ℚ, L = BPL, and the uniform form of Hilbert's sixteenth problem. Treat everything here as a set of preprints from an unknown author with an unusually strong partial certificate. It is not yet established mathematics.

02What was released

There are 722 manuscripts grouped into 372 "result families" across 17 subject areas. The README says they were produced by an unreleased internal OpenAI model, averaging three hours of ChatGPT Pro compute per result, from about 4,000 problems posed. Families were kept by "requiring an appropriate level of significance". Two items were produced outside that fixed procedure: the zeta zero-free region work and Hodge for CM abelian varieties. The 11/12 zeta write-up was human-edited. Manuscript directories are dated 10 September to 6 October 2026, mostly 23–27 September and 5 October; these look like write-up dates.

Ten families come with released reasoning summaries: 007, 017, 087, 102, 159, 197, 221, 271, 287, 362.

03How I rated

Each family is rated on importance if correct, using the coordinator's scale:

  • 1–2: yawn.
  • 3–4: a specialist paper.
  • 5–6: a named open problem of moderate fame.
  • 7–8: a major, decades-old, widely known conjecture.
  • 9: field-changing or prize-level.
  • 10: Millennium-scale.

I judged each problem's history and fame from my own knowledge and the catalogue's descriptions. I did not judge correctness. Verification is tagged separately from each family's Lean scope document (lean/docs/NNN.md), not from the catalogue's Lean badge (see below). Ratings are one reader's calibration. A specialist would move individual 6s, 7s and 8s by a point, but the shape of the distribution would not change.

05The top 20

1. Quasi-Riemann hypothesis (003): importance 10, Lean (headline). The claim is that ζ(s) and every Dirichlet L-function have no zeros with Re s > 7/8, the same holds for Hecke L-functions over ℚ(√−3), and there are no Landau–Siegel zeros. Before this, no fixed zero-free half-plane was known in the 167 years since Riemann. The best regions narrow toward Re s = 1, and Siegel zeros have blocked effective results for decades. I read the Comparator statement: it uses Mathlib's own riemannZeta, asserts riemannZeta s ≠ 0 for Re s > 7/8, and permits only the three standard axioms, so the statement is faithful. If the check passes, this is true. It is not the Riemann hypothesis, which needs 1/2, but it is the largest step toward it ever claimed. This was not produced by the standard procedure, and the alternate 11/12 write-up was human-edited.

2. Hilbert's sixteenth problem, uniform bound (143): importance 10. UNVERIFIED. The claim is that the number of limit cycles of a planar polynomial vector field is bounded by a function of its degree alone. This is unknown even for quadratic fields and is part of Hilbert's 1900 list and Smale's problems. The catalogue shows a Lean badge, but the Lean covers only quintic Liénard systems (at most two limit cycles). That is a good result on its own, but a different and far smaller one. The 10 rests entirely on an unformalized manuscript.

3. Full BSD formula in Selmer corank ≤ 1 (002): importance 9, unformalized. This proves the full Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer leading-term formula, including finiteness and order of Sha, for every elliptic curve over ℚ with Selmer corank 0 or 1 at some prime. Combined with Goldfeld's conjecture (006, rated 8, also unformalized), it gives BSD for 100% of quadratic twists. It covers most of BSD that anyone can currently reach. Rank ≥ 2 is untouched, so it is not the Millennium Problem. This is heavy Iwasawa theory, the kind of argument where gaps hide.

4. Hodge conjecture for CM abelian varieties (032): importance 9, unformalized. The claim is the rational Hodge conjecture for every CM abelian variety. Through Milne's theorems it would also give the Tate conjecture for all abelian varieties over finite fields and the Hodge standard conjecture for abelian varieties. Rational Hodge for products of K3 surfaces and algebraicity of Kuga–Satake come as companions. Before this, the best progress was recent work on Weil-type abelian fourfolds. Like quasi-RH, it was produced outside the fixed procedure, so how much is the model's work is unclear. The family's title (K3 surfaces) undersells its first sentence.

5. Unique Games Conjecture (102): importance 9, Lean (headline). Khot's conjecture, open since 2002. I read the formal statement. For every 0 < ε, δ < 1/2 it gives a polynomial-time reduction from 3SAT to unique games with affine constraints over 𝔽₂ˢ: satisfiable formulas map to games of value at least 1−ε, and unsatisfiable ones to games of value at most δ. This is a faithful (slightly stronger) form of UGC. Optimal hardness for Max-Cut (Goemans–Williamson) and factor-2 Vertex Cover are also formalized, by direct reductions. If it passes the check, it settles the approximability of a large class of problems.

6. Matrix multiplication ω ≤ 9/4 (107): importance 9, Lean (headline). The record before was about 2.3714, reached by decades of tiny improvements. I read the statement: algebraic programs using add, subtract and multiply that compute n×n products with O(n^{9/4+ε}) operations over ℂ. It also bounds ω < 2.3711 over every field. The result is purely asymptotic, but a jump this size would be the biggest in the subject since its founding results. Early readers on Hacker News called it "shocking".

7. No infinite cluster at criticality on ℤ³ (213): importance 9, Lean (headline). This is the defining open problem of percolation theory. It was known in 2D and in high dimensions, and d = 3 was the iconic gap. I read the statement, and it is faithful for both bond and site percolation. It also proves the Benjamini–Schramm conjecture for every quasi-transitive graph with p_c < 1.

8. Erdős's arithmetic-progression conjecture (159): importance 9, Lean (headline). The claim is that any set of integers whose reciprocals sum to infinity contains arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions. This is Erdős's best-known prize problem, previously reachable only for 3-term progressions after Kelley–Meka (2023). The formalized statement is exactly the reciprocal-sum conjecture. The quantitative Szemerédi bounds are unformalized.

9. Artin's primitive root conjecture (029): importance 9, unformalized. The claim is that every integer other than −1 and the squares is a primitive root mod infinitely many primes, with a lower bound of x/(log x)². The conjecture dates from 1927. It was known under GRH (Hooley 1967) and unconditionally with at most two exceptional bases (Heath-Brown 1986), but no single base, not even 2, was known.

10. Hilbert's tenth problem over ℚ (004): importance 9, unformalized. The claim is that no algorithm decides whether a polynomial with integer coefficients has a rational zero. This is the most famous open extension of Matiyasevich's theorem. Mazur's conjectures predict that ℤ has no existential definition in ℚ, so the proof must take a different route, and that route deserves close reading.

11. Free group factors are isomorphic (287): importance 9, Lean (headline). The claim is that L(F₂) ≅ L(F₃), and that all interpolated free group factors are isomorphic, with fundamental group ℝ₊. This is the best-known open problem about II₁ factors, settled in the direction many experts did not expect. I read the Comparator statement. It builds L(G) from scratch as the bicommutant of left translations on ℓ²(G), with the canonical trace and the ultraweak topology. It defines the interpolated factors as compressions of L(F₂) ⊗ B(ℓ²) by a projection of trace 1/√(r−1), and it asserts a normal, trace-preserving *-isomorphism for every pair r, s > 1, including ∞. To my reading this is faithful. It is also the most intricate definitional statement I checked, so it deserves a specialist's second look.

12. Thompson's group F is nonamenable (248): importance 9, Lean (headline). The problem has a history of announced proofs in both directions that were later withdrawn. I read the statement: F is defined as dyadic piecewise-linear homeomorphisms of [0,1], and the statement says there is no left-invariant mean on ℓ∞(F). It is faithful. Here a formal proof would be decisive in a way a manuscript could not be.

13. Cannon's conjecture (246): importance 9, Lean (headline). The claim is that a hyperbolic group with 2-sphere boundary acts geometrically on ℍ³. Equivalently, the hyperbolic 3-manifold groups are exactly these groups. This is central to geometric group theory and 3-manifold topology. I read the statement. Hyperbolicity is thin triangles in a Cayley graph. The boundary is defined as geodesic rays from the identity up to bounded distance, with the pointwise topology, which is a standard model. ℍ³ is the upper half-space with the arcosh distance. The conclusion is a proper, cocompact action with finite kernel. It looks faithful.

14. L = RL = BPL (103): importance 9, unformalized. Unconditional derandomization of log-space. The best before was Saks–Zhou (1999) and small improvements since. This would be a landmark of complexity theory.

15. Hadwiger's conjecture is false (157): importance 9. UNVERIFIED for the big part. The description says graphs with independence number 2 have fractional chromatic number above their clique-minor size. Since χ ≥ χ_f, that refutes Hadwiger's 1943 conjecture outright, a fact the title does not say. Only the positive companion (a linear list-coloring bound in the clique-minor size) is in Lean. The counterexample is unformalized.

16. A non-sofic group, via Kaplansky direct finiteness (197): importance 9, Lean (core claim). Lean formalizes a group algebra over a finite field of characteristic 2 where ab = 1 but ba ≠ 1. The group is finitely presented with odd torsion. Direct finiteness is known for every sofic group (Elek–Szabó), so a checked counterexample implies that a non-sofic group exists. Whether all groups are sofic is one of the big open questions in group theory. The family also disproves Gottschalk's surjunctivity conjecture and the group-ring Determinant Conjecture, with parts formalized. The "torsion-free" part of the title is not formalized.

17. Spontaneous magnetization of the quantum Heisenberg ferromagnet (271): importance 9, Lean (headline). For d ≥ 3, every spin, and low temperature, it constructs an infinite-volume KMS state with magnetization at least S/4. The ferromagnet, unlike the antiferromagnet, lacks reflection positivity, and the problem has been open since the 1970s. Bloch's T^{3/2} law with exact coefficient is claimed alongside.

18. Fourier restriction in ℝ³ (077): importance 9, unformalized. The claim is the extension estimate L^p → L^p for p > 3 for positively curved surfaces, which is Stein's restriction conjecture in three dimensions. It is central to harmonic analysis and implies the 3D Kakeya conjecture that Wang–Zahl proved in 2025.

19. Global smoothness for relativistic Vlasov–Maxwell (362): importance 9, Lean (headline). This is the Glassey–Strauss large-data problem (1986) in 3D. A PDE global-existence theorem in Lean would itself be unprecedented. I read the statement. It has the relativistic velocity v/√(1+|v|²), the Vlasov equation with the Lorentz force, Maxwell's equations with charge and current, both Gauss constraints, and smooth compactly supported data with finite-energy fields. It asserts global classical existence, smoothness on every finite interval, and uniqueness among classical solutions. It looks faithful.

20. Anderson localization and delocalization (261): importance 9. UNVERIFIED despite the badge. The claims are pure-point spectrum in 2D at every disorder strength, and absolutely continuous spectrum in d ≥ 3 at weak disorder (the extended-states conjecture). These are the central open problems of random Schrödinger operators. The Lean only identifies the spectrum as a set, which was already known. Neither the localization nor the delocalization claim is formalized.

06What is verified and what is not

What the Lean badge means. For a formalized family, a "Comparator challenge" holds the theorem statement with its proof replaced by sorry. The solution must prove exactly that statement using only Lean's three standard axioms. If the check passes, what remains to trust is:

  1. the Lean kernel and the Comparator tool;
  2. the statement file meaning what the paper claims.

Every challenge file I read imports only Mathlib, not OpenAI's own libraries, which is the right design. I read 16 of them, covering quasi-RH, Siegel zeros, UGC, ℤ³ percolation, Thompson F, ω, symmetric Mahler, the plane 7-coloring upper bound, a non-residually-finite hyperbolic group, Kaplansky direct finiteness, zero divisors, 2D O(n) decay, quintic Liénard, the free group factors, Cannon, and Vlasov–Maxwell. Each was, to my reading, a faithful formalization of what its scope document says it covers. That is a check of the statements, not of the proofs.

The badge overstates coverage. 235 families carry a Lean link, but by their own scope documents:

  • 189 formalize the headline claim;
  • 6 formalize the core claim with parts missing;
  • 40 formalize only a partial or supporting statement.

Among the 38 families rated 9 or 10:

  • 15 have the headline in Lean;
  • 3 have the core claim;
  • 4 have only partial or supporting results (Hilbert 16, Hadwiger, Anderson, self-avoiding walk);
  • 16 have nothing formal.

Independent checks so far.

  • Yongxi Lin (CMU PhD student, GitHub CoolRmal) ran the Comparator on unchanged sources in the real sandbox. For the Falconer distance conjecture (073), it passed at 00:07 UTC on 7 October ("Lean default kernel accepts the solution"). His run on the quasi-RH zeta statement (003) started at 00:32 UTC and was still in progress at 01:00 UTC.
  • One public fork review (isaksmith/math) reports no sorry anywhere in about 122,000 Lean files.
  • My own scan of the Lean sources I could check out found no axiom declarations. It also found no sorry in the number theory, computability, group theory and probability directories, about 45,000 files.
  • I could not build anything in this container.
  • The Comparator configs leave the second independent kernel checker (nanoda) off, and formalization.yaml marks review status "unchecked".
  • Nobody has yet publicly audited whether the formal statements match the papers. My own readings below are the only fidelity check I know of.

No error channel. The GitHub repository has issues and discussions disabled, so there is no public tracker for error reports.

08Strongest reasons for skepticism

  1. Volume and breadth. Dozens of the most famous open problems in nearly every field fell at once, in manuscripts dated within a few weeks. The base rate for long, novel, refereed-by-nobody proofs of famous conjectures being fully correct is low. Even if the error rate per manuscript is small, across 722 manuscripts and 137 unformalized families some will be wrong. The README says so itself.
  2. The biggest unformalized claims are the hardest to check. These include BSD, Hodge for CM abelian varieties, Artin, H10 over ℚ, L = BPL, 3D restriction, Hilbert–Smith, the Borel counterexample, the Haldane gap and uniform Hilbert 16. They are long arguments in areas where the reviewer pool is small.
  3. Lean is a certificate only of what was formalized, and only once someone runs it. Partial scopes sit behind full-looking badges (Hilbert 16, Hadwiger, Anderson, BEC at positive temperature, the self-avoiding-walk exponent, Lech, the Penrose inequality, the ionization bound). Statement fidelity has to be checked by humans claim by claim, especially where Mathlib lacks the objects: free group factors, Gromov boundaries, PDE function spaces. One result (mutually unbiased bases, N(6) = 3) rests on a floating-point computation outside Lean.
  4. Attribution is mixed. The two most famous items, quasi-RH and Hodge CM, were produced outside the fixed procedure, and one zeta write-up was human-edited. "The model did it" is not uniformly true.
  5. Selection with no denominator. About 4,000 problems were posed and 372 families were kept. No failed, wrong or abandoned attempts are reported, and there is no false-positive rate. The quoted compute is an average over successes.
  6. Five families are missing. Family numbers run to 377, but 045, 061, 070, 123 and 163 are absent, and the NYT and Quanta both report 377 results. Five results appear to have been removed before publication, with no reason given.
  7. Novelty and priority. Some results overlap existing or concurrent work. Families credit Carenini's concurrent k-SAT threshold and Tanaka's concurrent Naimark counterexample. Kakeya in 3D was proved by Wang–Zahl in 2025. A general Cartan–Hadamard proof was previously claimed by Ghomi–Spruck. A per-family literature check has not been done.
  8. Headline claims are buried. Several family titles understate their own contents. 157 refutes Hadwiger's conjecture. 197 implies non-sofic groups exist. 293 answers the transitive algebra problem. 032's title names K3 surfaces but leads with Hodge for CM abelian varieties. Buried claims get less scrutiny.
  9. Dependencies cascade. 034 depends on 033, 001 on 032, 096 on 102, and 002 on 006, so one error can take down several families.

09What this means

If the formally checked core survives an independent run and a human statement-fidelity review, the case that AI can produce research mathematics at scale is closed. The bottleneck moves from finding proofs to checking them. Three kinds of human work become the most valuable:

  • auditing that formal statements mean what the papers claim;
  • refereeing the unformalized flagship arguments;
  • extracting the new methods, since a proof of quasi-RH or UGC would carry techniques worth more than the theorem.

Nothing on that path requires taking OpenAI's word: anyone with the compute can run Comparator.

If the core does not survive, the failure will most likely be one of three things: a statement-fidelity gap, a Comparator or library soundness issue, or errors concentrated in the unformalized families. Three questions are worth watching now:

  • whether an independent group reports a clean Comparator run on the headline challenges;
  • whether specialists sign off on the formal statements for quasi-RH, UGC, ω, ℤ³ percolation and the free group factors;
  • what the first concrete error report is, and in which family.

The social context matters too. The release lands amid open conflict with parts of the mathematical community: an advisory group asked OpenAI to stop testing on open problems, and there was critical coverage in Wired, The Economist and Science in September. Expect the scrutiny to be intense, which is what the verification needs.
