The Backrooms

A walkthrough liminal-space attraction for the North Bay. No actors, no jump scares — you buy a slot, you go in alone with your group, and you come out twenty minutes later with photographs. This is the plan to actually build it, and the numbers say whether it is worth doing.

Draft, deliberately. Every figure here is a market rate or an estimate, not a quote. The purpose of this page is to be argued with — the numbers are laid out so a wrong one can be replaced without rebuilding the argument around it.

1. The idea in one paragraph

Timed-entry, self-guided, roughly twenty minutes end to end. Groups of about six go in every four minutes. There are no performers. The product is atmosphere and photographs — humming fluorescent tubes, damp carpet, yellow walls, corridors that turn back on themselves and rooms that are almost identical to rooms you were in a minute ago. It sells because people post it, which means the build has to photograph well above everything else.

Why this is cheaper to run than a haunted house. A conventional haunt is a payroll problem — twenty to forty performers a night. This has none. The cost is all in the build, which you pay once, and the staff are a handful of people running a door.

2. Site: take an office, not a warehouse

The instinct is a big empty warehouse. It is the wrong building, and the reason is the ceiling.

The Backrooms look is a suspended ceiling grid with fluorescent panels. In a bare warehouse you would have to build that grid underneath the existing sprinkler heads — which you may not do, because a solid ceiling under a sprinkler blocks its spray pattern and voids the system. You would be adding a second sprinkler layer below your false ceiling. That single detail has killed more themed builds than any other line item.

An office already has the grid, with the sprinkler heads already coming through it. It also already has exit signs, emergency lighting, a fire alarm, HVAC and restrooms. It is a building already designed for people to stand around inside.

Office is roughly two and a half times the rent per square foot and still the cheaper answer, because a warehouse only wins if you can build ceiling, lighting, sprinklers, HVAC and restrooms for less than the rent difference. Over a three-month run that difference is about $35,000. You cannot.

What to look for

  • A whole building or a whole floor. Never a suite. You will have queues, noise and strangers; a landlord with a title company two doors down will not wear it, and shared corridors are someone else's fire escape.
  • Still fitted out. Resist the clean open floor plate. A floor still carrying its cubicle-era partitions, carpet and ceiling tiles is most of your set already. A landlord who stripped it to shell has done you a disservice and will still charge you.
  • Check partitions run to deck. Office walls often stop at the grid. That changes how light and sound bleed between rooms, which is most of the atmosphere.
  • Count the parking. Assembly use needs more stalls per square foot than office does. Usually fine on a suburban office park, but it is a plan-check item.

Target: about 6,000 square feet. Big enough for a twenty-minute folded path, small enough that the rent does not eat the project. See the numbers below — 10,000 square feet over three months does not break even.

3. Permits: this is the critical path

Under the California Fire Code this is a special amusement area. A special amusement area is an assembly occupancy regardless of occupant load. There is no version of this small enough to avoid the classification.

An office is Group B. You are asking for a change of occupancy to assembly, which brings:

  • Automatic fire sprinklers throughout — present already in most offices, which is the point of choosing one
  • Smoke detection throughout, alarming at a constantly attended location
  • A special amusement operational permit, minimum two weeks before opening
  • Egress, exit marking and emergency lighting to assembly standard
  • Plan review of your walls — a maze changes exit paths, and that is the thing they will actually scrutinise

Ring the fire marshal before signing anything. It is free, it takes an afternoon, and their answer should decide which city you lease in. Vallejo and American Canyon are separate jurisdictions with separate plan reviewers and separate opinions.

5. Does it make money?

Ticket at $35, plus about $6 a head in photo prints and merchandise. Capacity is 540 a night — groups of six, one every four minutes, six hours. Eighteen nights across October: eight Friday/Saturday, six Thursday/Sunday, four in Halloween week.

The number that matters is sell-through, and it is the number nobody can promise you.

Break-even is about 3,050 visitors. That is the whole case: the conservative scenario roughly washes its face, and everything above it is profit. You are not betting the project on a good October — you are betting it on a mediocre one.
The version that fails. 10,000 sf on a three-month lease at $32 a ticket loses about $36,000 even at a decent 70% sell-through. The extra 4,000 square feet cost rent and buildout without adding a single ticket, because throughput is set by how often you release a group, not by floor area. Resist the urge to take the bigger unit.

6. Building it

Walls

Timber stud with a single skin is enough — nobody leans on them, and one side is all anyone sees. Build in repeating modules so corridors can be reconfigured after the first weekend tells you where people bunch up. Leave the tops open to the grid where the code officer allows it; the light spill over the top of a wall is a large part of the feeling.

The yellow

Get this wrong and nothing else matters. It is not a clean yellow — it is a stained, slightly green, fluorescent-lit wallpaper colour. Test large samples under the actual tubes you will install, because the lamps shift it considerably. Paint big boards, not chips.

Light

The lighting is the attraction. Cool white tubes, some deliberately failing, one or two with an audible ballast hum. Do not light it evenly or it stops being unsettling — you want pools and long dim runs between them. Keep every fixture within the electrical allowance of the building you lease and get an electrician to sign it off.

Sound

A continuous low hum, some rooms slightly out of phase with others. Almost no dynamics. The silence between hums is what people report afterwards.

The photograph problem

People will stop to take pictures, which throttles throughput and creates bunching. Design two or three obvious photo rooms — wider, better lit, unmistakably the shot — and keep the corridors between them narrow and unrewarding. You are choosing where the queue forms rather than pretending it will not.

7. Marketing

Fifteen thousand dollars, and the largest single line after the lease. It is also the line most likely to be cut and the one that decides whether the conservative or the good column happens.

  • The build is the campaign. Film the construction from week one. Empty office to finished corridor is inherently watchable and costs nothing but the phone already in your pocket.
  • Give it away early. Local creators, in free, before opening, on the explicit understanding they post. Their footage is the advertisement — this is a format that sells entirely on other people's photographs.
  • Timed tickets create the urgency for you. "Saturday 8pm — 4 slots left" does more than any ad. Sell in slots from day one, visibly.
  • Spend locally. Vallejo, Napa, Fairfield, Benicia, plus the Bay Area photography and cosplay communities, who are the core audience and will travel.
  • Have a hashtag and a printed sign asking for it. Unglamorous, works.

8. Running it

Six on shift
RolenDoing what
Door / ticket scan1Checks slots, manages the queue outside
Dispatcher1Releases groups on the clock — the single most important job
Floor2Inside, unobtrusive, watching for bunching and for anyone unwell
Exit / merch1Photo prints, shirts, the last impression
Manager1Incidents, refunds, the fire marshal if they drop in
  • The dispatcher sets the whole experience. Release too fast and people see the group ahead, which destroys the isolation the product depends on. Too slow and you lose revenue. Four minutes is a starting guess, not a fixed rule — tune it on night one.
  • Nobody goes in alone unless they ask to. Solo walkthroughs are the best version of this and also where your incidents come from. Offer it, staff it, and log it.
  • Publish an accessibility route. Some of your audience will not do a twenty-minute walk with low light and uneven floor. Say so plainly on the ticket page.
  • Count everyone in and out. Assembly occupancy, and you will be asked.
