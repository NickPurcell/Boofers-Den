Brain health · a practical guide

Lowering your Alzheimer’s risk. What actually works.

If someone you love has Alzheimer’s, wondering about your own odds is a reasonable thing to do. This page takes that seriously: what a family history means in real numbers, what lowers risk, how to approach genetic tests and a parent’s diagnosis, and which viral claims hold up. Every claim links to its source and says how strong the evidence is.

Written 30 October 2026, from papers, guidelines and regulator documents published up to October 2026.

This is a reading guide, not medical advice. Your doctor or a genetic counselor can apply it to you, and they should be the ones to interpret any test.

01The short version

  1. Your worry is reasonable, and the real numbers are gentler than they feel. About 1 in 5 women develop Alzheimer’s dementia in their lifetime (Chêne 2015). A parent diagnosed before about 80 raises that, to something like 1 in 3, perhaps up to 2 in 5, for a woman over a long life Our estimate. Most of that risk arrives after 75, and most children of a parent with Alzheimer’s never develop it.
  2. Late-onset cases count for much less. A parent, grandparent, aunt or uncle who developed Alzheimer’s in their mid-80s or later adds little or nothing measurable on its own; at that age it is common (Wolters 2017) (Silverman 2003).
  3. What you do still matters, whatever your genes. Know your blood pressure, cholesterol and blood sugar, stay off nicotine, keep moving, protect your hearing, and drink little. Treating high blood pressure is the one step proven in trials to prevent dementia (He 2025), and all of them protect your heart. In trials, people carrying the APOE e4 risk gene got as much benefit from lifestyle programs as everyone else (Baker 2025).
  4. The rare inherited form looks distinctive. Single-gene Alzheimer’s is 1% or less of cases. It shows up as several relatives across generations, typically starting in their 30s to 50s (Alzheimer’s Association 2026).
  5. Genetic testing is optional, and the order matters. See a genetic counselor first, sort out life and disability insurance before testing (US law doesn’t protect those), and where possible test the affected relative first. The prevention advice is the same whatever a test says.
  6. Skip Alzheimer’s blood tests while you feel fine. They are cleared only for people with symptoms, and in young, healthy people a “positive” is more likely to be wrong than right (section 07).
  7. If a parent has memory changes, help them get checked. Some contributors are treatable, today’s treatments only work early, and their diagnosis tells you more about your own picture than any test you could take.
  8. The sauna story is a stretch. Doug Whitney is real and remarkable, but the paper about him never mentions saunas, and the human sauna evidence is thin.
  9. You can join the research today. Free registries take adults from 18 and put you in line for future prevention studies.

How to read the evidence tags

  • StrongRandomized trials, meta-analyses, or very large population studies.
  • ModerateGood observational studies, or one large study with real limits.
  • WeakSmall, early, lab-only, or a single case.
  • GuidelineWhat expert bodies, regulators or the law say.
  • Our estimateOur own arithmetic or summary from the studies cited. Not a published figure.

02What you can do, ranked

Here’s the encouraging part. A large share of dementia is tied to things people can change, and most of them are in your hands.

If you’re a young adult, the job is to set your course, not to fix anything. Dementia at ages 30 to 34 affects about 1 in 100,000 people (Hendriks 2021). Readings of blood pressure and weight taken decades before any symptoms are the ones that predict dementia (Abell 2018) (Kivimäki 2018), and the Lancet Commission counts “midlife” from age 18 (Livingston 2024).

What “45% preventable” means

The 2024 Lancet Commission estimates that about 45% of dementia is linked to 14 changeable factors (Livingston 2024). That figure is a model of whole populations, and it assumes every factor is a cause. It doesn’t mean one person can remove 45% of their own risk; the Commission itself says it “does not guarantee any individual will avoid dementia”. The honest version: these habits shift the odds and probably push dementia later.

How sure is the science?

Only one change has randomized-trial proof that it prevents dementia itself: treating high blood pressure. In a trial of 33,995 adults with high blood pressure, treatment cut dementia by 15% (He 2025), and pooled earlier trials agree (Peters 2022). Strong · randomized trials

Programs that combine exercise, diet, brain training and health checks improved thinking scores in people aged 60 to 79 (Ngandu 2015) (Baker 2025), whether or not they carried APOE e4 (Solomon 2018). No lifestyle trial has yet shown fewer dementia diagnoses. Most of the other numbers below come from observational studies, which can overstate benefits. Strong · trials, thinking scores

Higher inherited risk doesn’t cancel the benefit; the same habits help on top of a higher starting point. In UK Biobank, among people over 60 whose genes put them at high risk, 1.13% of those with a healthy lifestyle developed dementia over about 8 years, against 1.78% of those with an unhealthy one (Lourida 2019). That is about a third lower in relative terms, 0.65 percentage points in absolute terms, and similar at every level of genetic risk. Moderate · observational

And whatever their exact effect on the brain, most of the steps below also lower the risk of heart disease, stroke and diabetes, which is well established.

Ranked by what’s most worth doing now

  1. Know your blood pressure, cholesterol and blood sugar

    How: a blood pressure check every 3–5 years while it’s normal (under 120/80) and you’re under 40, and yearly from 40 or if you’re at higher risk (USPSTF 2021). A cholesterol test every 4–6 years (CDC). A blood sugar (HbA1c) test early if you have risk factors such as a family history of diabetes, past gestational diabetes or PCOS (USPSTF 2021). Act on numbers early: the Lancet Commission’s target is a top number of 130 or less from age 40 (Livingston 2024).

    Why: blood pressure is the one factor with trial proof against dementia (He 2025). A top number of 130 or more at age 50 was linked to 38% higher dementia risk later (Abell 2018). High LDL before 65 predicted dementia more than 10 years later (Iwagami 2021), and diabetes that starts earlier carries more risk, so delaying it counts (Barbiellini Amidei 2021).

    Strong · trials (blood pressure) Moderate · cholesterol, sugar

  2. No nicotine

    How: quit completely rather than cutting down. There’s no dementia data on vaping yet; treat it as a step off nicotine, not a safe alternative.

    Why: people who smoke have about 30% higher dementia risk, and former smokers’ risk is back to that of people who never smoked (Zhong 2015).

    Moderate · observational

  3. Move, and build some strength

    How: 150–300 minutes a week of moderate activity, or 75–150 vigorous, plus two strength sessions (WHO 2020). Short bouts count: the WHO dropped its old 10-minute minimum, because activity of any length adds up (Bull 2020). The winning program in the POINTER trial used four 30–35 minute aerobic sessions and two short strength sessions a week, with a group to keep people going (Baker 2025).

    Why: fitness reliably improves blood pressure, blood sugar, weight and cholesterol. Its direct link to dementia is less certain than headlines suggest: active people get less dementia, but part of that is early disease making people less active, and the link largely disappears when activity is measured more than 10 years before diagnosis (Kivimäki 2019).

    Moderate · observational Strong · for heart and metabolism

  4. Protect your hearing

    How: keep headphone volume under 60%, use noise-cancelling headphones, wear earplugs at concerts and loud classes (WHO), and get a hearing test if you notice ringing or loss. Treat hearing loss early.

    Why: hearing loss is joint-largest of the Lancet’s 14 factors (Livingston 2024), and noise damage is permanent but avoidable. In the ACHIEVE trial, hearing care didn’t slow decline overall in 70–84-year-olds, but slowed it by 48% in a higher-risk group (Lin 2023). Using hearing aids before 70 was linked to lower dementia risk (Francis 2025).

    Moderate · trial subgroup and cohorts

  5. Keep alcohol low, and never binge

    How: well under 14 UK units (about 8 US drinks) a week, and less is better. Never drink to the point of blacking out. Don’t start drinking for “heart health”.

    Why: drinking to loss of consciousness was linked to about double the dementia risk (Kivimäki 2020), and genetic studies find no protective amount (Topiwala 2025).

    Moderate · observational and genetic

  6. Protect sleep where you can, and get snoring checked

    How: aim for a regular 7–8 hour window when life allows. Ask for a sleep study if you snore loudly, someone notices pauses in your breathing, or sleep never feels refreshing. Therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is the first-line treatment (Qaseem 2016). Don’t make “PM” sleep aids with diphenhydramine or doxylamine a habit (Gray 2015).

    Why: people who usually slept 6 hours or less in their 50s and 60s had about 20–40% higher dementia risk (Sabia 2021), and sleep apnea is linked to higher risk too (Guay-Gagnon 2022). The Lancet Commission couldn’t make a sleep recommendation, but says people “should not curtail their sleep” (Livingston 2024).

    Weak · observational

    Up at night with a baby or young kids?

    Those studies measured people’s usual sleep in their 50s and 60s, not a season of night feeds and early wake-ups. Sleep when you can, get snoring checked, and don’t add worry about your brain to being tired. Our reading

  7. Get help for low mood and anxiety

    How: if low mood or anxiety hangs around, get help: talk therapy, medication, or both. Check in with yourself after big transitions, such as a new baby or perimenopause.

    Why: depression is one of the Lancet’s 14 factors (Livingston 2024). Among 354,313 UK adults, people whose depression was treated had about 30% lower dementia rates than people whose depression went untreated (Yang 2023). Treat it because you deserve to feel better; any benefit to the brain is a bonus.

    Moderate · observational

  8. Eat Mediterranean-style

    How: olive oil as your everyday fat; leafy greens, beans, nuts and berries; fish twice a week; processed meat and packaged snacks now and then; water, coffee or tea as your usual drinks.

    Why: across 23 cohort studies, people eating Mediterranean-style had 11% lower dementia risk (Fekete 2025), and more ultra-processed food went with higher risk (Li 2022). But a 3-year trial of the MIND diet in 70-year-olds found no difference, partly because both groups improved (Barnes 2023). The sure benefit is to blood pressure, cholesterol and weight.

    Moderate · observational; trial null

  9. Wear a helmet

    How: for bikes, e-scooters, skiing, snowboarding and riding; always buckle up. After a suspected concussion, stop for the day and get checked.

    Why: in 2.8 million Danes, having had a head injury was linked to 24% higher dementia risk, and five or more to nearly three times the risk (Fann 2018).

    Moderate · observational

  10. Keep learning

    How: take on a structured project each year (a language, an instrument, a course), and lean toward work that stretches you. Puzzles and apps are fine for fun.

    Why: education is one of the few factors backed by genetic studies (Larsson 2017), and people in mentally stimulating jobs had 23% lower dementia risk, a link that held beyond 10 years (Kivimäki 2021).

    Moderate · genetic and observational

  11. Keep regular plans with people

    How: recurring, in-person commitments: a class, a team, a choir, volunteering, a standing friend date.

    Why: loneliness is linked to about 31% higher dementia risk (Luchetti 2024), though part of that is early disease causing withdrawal. It’s low-cost and good for mood regardless.

    Weak · observational

  12. Clean up indoor air on bad days

    How: run a HEPA purifier in the bedroom on high-pollution or wildfire-smoke days, and use the range hood when cooking. Don’t give up exercise to avoid ordinary city air.

    Why: fine-particle pollution is linked to 8% higher dementia risk per 5 µg/m³ (Best Rogowski 2025). The per-person effect is modest.

    Weak · observational

For women specifically

  • Pregnancy complications are a lifelong flag. If you’ve had preeclampsia, high blood pressure in pregnancy or gestational diabetes, keep it on your medical record and check blood pressure yearly, and blood sugar and cholesterol regularly, for life (Parikh 2021). Preeclampsia is linked to higher vascular dementia risk (Basit 2018). In 1.37 million women, the link largely disappeared once later high blood pressure and diabetes were accounted for, which suggests catching and treating those is what matters (Seong 2026). Moderate · observational
  • Early menopause or ovary removal. Losing ovarian hormones early is linked to higher dementia risk (Rocca 2007) (Gong 2022). If periods stop before about 40–45, or both ovaries are removed before menopause, ask about estrogen until around 52, the usual age of menopause. If a hysterectomy is offered for a benign reason, ask whether the ovaries can stay (Menopause Society 2022). Moderate · observational Guideline
  • Hormone therapy is for symptoms, not for the brain. Started around menopause, it was neutral for thinking in trials (Gleason 2024). Started at 65 or later, it doubled dementia (Shumaker 2003). The Menopause Society says it is “not recommended at any age to prevent or treat a decline in cognitive function or dementia” (Menopause Society 2022). Strong · randomized trials

Later in life, worth remembering

  • Shingles vaccine from 50. Two natural experiments found fewer new dementia diagnoses after shingles vaccination; in Wales the effect was concentrated in women (Eyting 2025) (Pomirchy 2025). Moderate · natural experiments
  • Treat hearing and vision loss when they come, with hearing aids or cataract surgery (Livingston 2024).
  • Don’t start hormone therapy at 65 or later in the hope of protecting your brain (above).

03What a family history means, in numbers

A family history is real, measurable risk. It is also smaller, and arrives later, than it feels when you’re worried.

Start with the baseline

For a woman who reaches 45, the lifetime risk of Alzheimer’s dementia is about 1 in 5; for a man it’s about 1 in 10 (Chêne 2015). The Alzheimer’s Association still uses this figure in its 2026 report (Alzheimer’s Association 2026). Moderate · one long-running US cohort

Most of that risk comes late. Alzheimer’s dementia affects about 5% of Americans aged 65 to 74, 14% of those 75 to 84, and 36% of those 85 and older (Alzheimer’s Association 2026). Strong · population estimates

Women make up almost two-thirds of US cases, mostly because women live longer. The same report says it is “not clear” that the risk differs between men and women of the same age.

If you’ve seen bigger numbers

A 2025 US study put the lifetime risk of dementia from any cause, between 55 and 95, at 42% (Fang 2025). That is a different measure: every kind of dementia, not only Alzheimer’s, counted out to age 95. It can’t be compared with the 1 in 5 above, and family-history multipliers can’t be applied to it.

One affected parent or sibling

Studies that follow whole populations forward find that having one parent or sibling with Alzheimer’s raises your risk about 1.7 times (Cannon-Albright 2019) (Wolters 2017). Two or more affected parents or siblings is about 4 times (Cannon-Albright 2019). Moderate · large population studies

Older studies that start from patients and ask about their families give bigger numbers, 2.6 to 7.5 times. Those are inflated, because the families of patients remember and report more illness (van Duijn 1991). Weak · recall bias

In absolute terms, a large US family study found that 27 in 100 close relatives (parents, siblings and children) of people with Alzheimer’s had developed dementia by 85, against 10 in 100 of the patients’ spouses; among women relatives it was 31 in 100. Those figures are for the study’s white families; African American families had higher rates, for relatives and spouses alike (Green 2002). That is probably an overestimate: the patients came from memory clinics, and the method ignores other causes of death. Even so, about 7 in 10 women relatives were free of dementia at 85. Moderate · family study, likely high

The age it started matters a lot

In the Rotterdam Study, a parent diagnosed with dementia before 80 was linked to about 2.6 times the risk. A parent diagnosed at 80 or later was linked to no measurable increase (Wolters 2017). Moderate · population cohort

Grandparents, aunts and uncles count for less. In a Utah study of 270,818 people with linked family trees, risk from affected second-degree relatives was above average but “typically not significantly elevated”; the one exception was aunts and uncles on the mother’s side (Cannon-Albright 2019). With 36% of people over 85 having Alzheimer’s dementia, a grandparent who developed it late in life is common.

About “roughly 1 in 3”

No study measures risk for every combination of relatives. “Roughly 1 in 3 to 2 in 5” is our translation of the studies above for a parent diagnosed before 80; it is not a published figure. You can’t get it by multiplying 1 in 5 by a relative risk, because those ratios come from different outcomes and time spans. The studies that counted relatives directly found about 22 to 31 in 100 had Alzheimer’s or another dementia by age 85 (Silverman 2003) (Green 2002), and the rest of a long life adds more. Nobody can put a precise number on one family.

Mother’s side or father’s side?

The evidence is mixed. A 2024 study of 4,413 healthy older adults found more amyloid on brain scans in people whose mother had memory problems, but “no association with cognition” (Seto 2024). The Rotterdam Study found no difference between a mother’s and a father’s history (Wolters 2017), and an older family study found the reverse (Lautenschlager 1996). Mothers also live longer and get diagnosed more often. It isn’t a reason to add a multiplier. Weak · mixed results

“58–79% genetic” doesn’t mean what it sounds like

Twin studies estimate that 58% to 79% of the variation in late-onset Alzheimer’s across a population is linked to genes (Gatz 2006). That describes differences across a population. It isn’t your personal chance, and it doesn’t mean your risk is fixed. The same authors say nongenetic factors “might be the focus for interventions” (Gatz 2006), and families share habits and surroundings as well as genes (Alzheimer’s Association 2026).

04Is it the rare inherited kind?

A small number of families carry a single-gene form of Alzheimer’s. It is the scenario people fear most. It is rare, and it has a recognizable shape.

Mutations in three genes, APP, PSEN1 and PSEN2, cause 1% or less of all Alzheimer’s (Alzheimer’s Association 2026). Each child of a carrier has a 50% chance of inheriting the mutation, and carriers almost always develop the disease (GeneReviews). Symptoms start on average around 45 for PSEN1, 52 for APP and 60 for PSEN2, which varies more (Liu 2025). Strong · registry and consensus data

Patterns that suggest it

  • Three or more affected relatives across at least two generations (Goldman 2011).
  • Onset that is consistently early within the family, typically in the 30s to 50s.
  • Carriers in one family usually start within about 5 years of the age their parent did (Ryman 2014).
  • A known mutation in any relative.
  • A single case at around 50 or younger, even with no family history, because new mutations happen (Lanoiselée 2017).

Patterns that argue against it

  • A single late-onset case, or affected relatives only in their 80s.
  • Very different onset ages in the same line of the family.
  • Several unaffected relatives already past the age the family’s cases began.

Guideline From the genetics guideline (Goldman 2011) and onset studies (Ryman 2014).

Even when Alzheimer’s starts before 65, most people don’t carry one of these mutations. The Alzheimer’s Association puts it at about 10 in 100 (Alzheimer’s Association 2026). A 2024 French study that gene-tested 608 patients whose symptoms began at 65 or younger found one in about 3 to 4 in 100 (Nicolas 2024). Strong · prospective gene testing

If a parent is diagnosed with Alzheimer’s that began before 65, their specialists may offer them genetic testing, and some experts now offer it to every early-onset patient (Nicolas 2024). A negative result in the affected parent largely settles the question for the whole family.

Not every dementia is Alzheimer’s

Frontotemporal dementia (FTD) usually strikes younger: about 60% of people with it are 45 to 60 (Alzheimer’s Association 2026), and it changes behaviour or language before memory. Its genetics are different: about 30% of people with FTD have a strong family history (Greaves & Rohrer 2019). That is one reason the exact diagnosis matters to the family (section 08).

05APOE, the common risk gene

APOE is the gene people mean when they say “the Alzheimer’s gene”. Everyone has two copies, each one of three types: e2, e3 or e4. An e4 raises risk. It doesn’t decide it.

Between 1 in 4 and 1 in 3 people of European ancestry carry at least one e4, and about 2 in 100 carry two (Lumsden 2020). How common it is varies a lot between ancestries (Ensembl/gnomAD).

Estimates built a different way, from case–control studies, run higher for women: about 30% with one e4 and 60% with two (Genin 2011). A family history adds to these numbers. And most of this data comes from people of European ancestry; the size of the e4 effect differs between ancestry groups (Belloy 2023).

A risk gene, not a sentence

  • About half of people with Alzheimer’s have no e4 at all (Ward 2012).
  • Most people with one e4 don’t develop mild cognitive impairment or dementia by 85, and about half of those with two don’t (Reiman 2025).
  • Even with two copies, symptoms typically begin in the mid-60s or later (Fortea 2024).
  • The e2 type is protective (Reiman 2020).

Healthy habits still work if you carry e4

In the FINGER trial, people with an e4 got at least as much benefit to their thinking from a two-year lifestyle program as people without one (Solomon 2018). In the 2,111-person US POINTER trial, “cognitive benefits did not differ between APOE ε4 carriers and noncarriers” (Baker 2025). The 2024 Lancet Commission concluded that dementia risk “is modifiable irrespective of APOE genetic status” (Livingston 2024). Moderate · trial subgroups

Two honest caveats. Some observational studies find a weaker link between healthy habits and lower risk in e4 carriers, and one found none (Licher 2019). And these trials measured thinking scores over about two years in people over 60. No trial has yet shown that lifestyle changes prevent dementia itself, in anyone.

The 2024 “distinct form” headline

A 2024 paper found that almost everyone with two e4 copies develops Alzheimer’s brain changes by later life, and argued that two copies should count as a genetic form of the disease (Fortea 2024). It is contested: population studies still put the clinical risk by 85 at 30–55% for two copies (Reiman 2025), and guidelines haven’t adopted the idea. Its real effect so far is on treatment. Europe approved lecanemab and donanemab only for people with zero or one e4 (EMA), because brain swelling on treatment is far more common with two copies: about 33% versus 5% with no e4 on lecanemab (Honig 2024).

Polygenic scores

Scores that add up many small genetic effects add little beyond APOE for predicting one person’s risk (Stocker 2023). They work less well outside the ancestry they were built on (Osterman 2024), and no guideline supports using them in the clinic.

06Genetic testing, done well

Testing is a decision about information, not a gate to prevention: what lowers risk is the same whatever a test says. If you’d still like to know, these steps protect you.

1. Talk to a genetic counselor first

Guidelines don’t recommend APOE testing to predict Alzheimer’s in healthy people, and say anyone who still wants it should have counseling before and after (Goldman 2011). A counselor helps you decide whether to test at all, which test, and what each possible result would mean for you and your relatives. Guideline

The National Society of Genetic Counselors keeps a searchable directory with a telehealth option (NSGC). Self-pay telehealth first visits cost around $250, for example at the nonprofit Genetic Support Foundation. Insurance rarely pays for predictive APOE testing. Weak · prices from provider pages APOE results can be given safely by phone: a trial found it worked as well as in person (Christensen 2018). That doesn’t apply to testing for the rare inherited genes, where guidelines call for results in person (Goldman 2011).

2. Sort out insurance before you test

In the US, the Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act (GINA) stops health insurers, and employers with 15 or more staff, from using genetic information, and that includes your family medical history. It does not cover life insurance, long-term care insurance or disability insurance, and the military is exempt (genome.gov). Law

Alzheimer’s blood-test results such as p-tau or amyloid aren’t genetic tests, so GINA likely doesn’t cover them at all (29 CFR 1635.3). As of May 2026, Florida was the only state banning genetic underwriting in life and long-term care insurance (Nasir 2026).

Life insurers generally rely on family history and medical records rather than asking for genetic tests, but in most states they could legally use results (Nasir 2026), and every question on an application has to be answered truthfully (NAIC). So a common piece of advice is to put any life or disability cover you want in place before testing, “although this approach has limitations” (Prince & Roche 2014). This isn’t legal or financial advice; a counselor can walk you through it.

3. Test the affected relative first, if you can

Genetic testing tells a family the most when it starts with the person who has the disease (Goldman 2011). If they carry a mutation, relatives can be tested for that exact change, and a negative result is truly reassuring. Testing a healthy relative first can’t do that: a negative could mean you didn’t inherit it, or that the family’s cause is somewhere the test didn’t look. Guideline

One new wrinkle: before a parent starts lecanemab or donanemab, their doctors test APOE, and the treatment guidance says to counsel patients on what it means for family members (Rabinovici 2025) (Leqembi label). A parent’s treatment can tell the family something genetic, so it’s worth talking about beforehand.

Consumer DNA tests and raw data files

23andMe’s FDA-authorized Alzheimer’s report checks only for e4, can’t report the rare inherited genes, and is “not for medical decisions” (FDA 2017). The raw data files you can download from these services are less reliable than they look:

  • APOE needs two positions, rs429358 and rs7412. A file missing either one can’t answer the question, and “missing” doesn’t mean “normal” (dbSNP).
  • It depends on the company. 23andMe files usually contain both positions. AncestryDNA files reportedly can’t give a reliable APOE answer: they reported no e4 even for a known carrier (Batra & Huang 2020). Moderate · one comparison
  • Rare “disease gene” hits are mostly false. For very rare variants on these chips, only 16% were confirmed by sequencing, and 20 of 21 people with consumer data had at least one false-positive rare variant (Weedon 2021). So raw data can neither find nor rule out the inherited Alzheimer’s genes. Strong
  • A relative’s file is about everyone. If a parent carries one e4, each child has a 50% chance of inheriting it, so reading one person’s file reveals odds for their relatives. Ask the owner first, offer results rather than announcing them, and don’t look up children.
  • If you do look: sort out insurance and see a counselor first (Uhlmann 2026), and confirm any result with a clinical test before acting on it (NSGC). We’d also look up the two positions offline rather than uploading the file to a website. Our suggestion
Company changes and deleting your data

23andMe went through bankruptcy in 2025, and its data now sits with a nonprofit, 23andMe Research Institute. In 2026, 43 attorneys general secured an $18M settlement and new security terms (NY Attorney General 2026). You can still download your data first and then delete it in your account settings; data already used in research can’t be withdrawn.

Third-party interpretation sites rely on old databases and can show an APOE result without warning, and some keep or share uploads. Uploading a file to a matching database puts your relatives’ DNA there too.

How people feel afterwards

In the REVEAL trial, adult children of people with Alzheimer’s who learned their APOE status had no more anxiety, depression or distress over a year than those who didn’t, and people who learned they had no e4 felt better (Green 2009). A much larger 2025 study of 9,496 people found small, short-lived rises in distress, mostly in people with two e4 copies (Langbaum 2025). Strong · trial and large cohort

Two catches. These were counseled, screened volunteers, and people who were already distressed did worse. People who learned results with no preparation describe real distress (Zallen 2018). Before deciding, it helps to answer:

  • Why do I want to know, and what would I do differently with each result?
  • How is my mood right now?
  • Who will come with me, and who will I tell?
  • Who in my family might not want to know? A result says something about parents and siblings too.
  • Have I sorted out insurance?
  • Do I know I can stop at any point, including not opening a result?
If a family mutation is ever confirmed

If a relative is found to carry an APP, PSEN1 or PSEN2 mutation, there are options for future children. IVF with embryo testing (PGT-M) can select embryos without the mutation. A version called exclusion testing does this without telling the at-risk parent their own status. Donor eggs or sperm and adoption are other routes (ASRM 2024) (ESHRE 2020). The US fertility society calls PGT-M “ethically justifiable” for conditions like this and recommends an experienced genetic counselor. Children themselves shouldn’t be tested (Goldman 2011).

07Alzheimer’s blood tests: not while you feel fine

New blood tests for p-tau217 and amyloid are a real advance for diagnosing people with symptoms. For healthy people, every guideline says not yet.

As of October 2026 the FDA has cleared four Alzheimer’s blood tests. Every one is only for people who already have symptoms of cognitive decline, with minimum ages of 40, 50 or 55, and none is cleared for screening (FDA 2025) (Roche 2025) (C2N 2026) (Roche 2026). Regulator

The Alzheimer’s Association “recommend[s] against diagnostic testing in cognitively unimpaired individuals” outside research, and the International Working Group agrees (Jack 2024) (Dubois 2024). The 2025 blood-test guideline warns that direct-to-consumer tests “can lead to confusion, misinterpretation, or unwarranted anxiety” (Palmqvist 2025). Guideline

Why not, if you’re young and well

  • The changes they detect are rare in young adults. Amyloid starts building 20 to 30 years before symptoms. Even among healthy people with two e4 copies, it isn’t until about age 40 that 15% test positive, and data for people under 50 are sparse (Jansen 2015).
  • So a positive is more likely wrong than right. If 1 to 2 in 100 people had amyloid and a test were 90% accurate both ways, only about 8 to 16 in 100 positive results would be true. Our estimate · arithmetic
  • No cutoffs have been checked at young ages, and kidney problems and other conditions can push results up.
  • Nothing approved can be done with a positive. Today’s treatments are only for people with symptoms.
  • A result isn’t protected by GINA (section 06).

Where these tests do help is a parent with symptoms who is being evaluated. In one study of patients with memory complaints, a blood test identified Alzheimer’s correctly about 91% of the time, against 61% for primary-care doctors working without one (Palmqvist 2024). Strong · prospective study

08Helping a parent get evaluated

If a parent is showing memory or thinking changes, helping them get a proper evaluation is one of the most useful things you can do, for them and for your own picture.

Why it’s worth the hard conversation

  • Treatable contributors get found. Thyroid problems, vitamin deficiencies, depression, sleep apnea, medication side effects and alcohol can all make thinking worse (Alzheimer’s Association 2026), and the evaluation guideline adds B12 tests and hearing (Atri 2025). Be realistic: these “rarely cause but may often contribute” (Atri 2025). In one review, 9% of dementia cases had a potentially reversible cause, but under 1% actually reversed (Clarfield 2003).
  • The type of dementia changes what the family should do. When symptoms start before 65, the guideline calls for prompt specialist referral, because the range of possible causes is wider (Atri 2025). That includes frontotemporal dementia, which is far more often inherited.
  • Their diagnosis tells you more than any test you could take. Testing the affected person first is the most informative genetic route for the whole family (section 06).
  • Treatment only works early. Lecanemab and donanemab are approved only for mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia with confirmed amyloid. In trials they slowed decline by about 27% (lecanemab) and 22–35% (donanemab) over 18 months, with real risks of brain swelling or bleeding (van Dyck 2023) (Sims 2023). A 2026 Cochrane review called the benefit trivial, and other experts disputed how it pooled the trials (Cochrane 2026) (Science Media Centre 2026). Either way, a late diagnosis closes the window.
  • Planning while they can take part. Powers of attorney and healthcare wishes need legal capacity, and money trouble such as missed bills can show up up to 6 years before a diagnosis (Nicholas 2021).
  • It takes a while. It is 3.5 years on average from first symptoms to diagnosis, and 4.1 years when dementia starts young (Kusoro 2025). In a 2026 US survey, the wait for a first specialist memory appointment averaged about 18 weeks (Knox 2026). Starting with their regular doctor gets the tests moving while they wait.

The pattern that matters is new, persistent, getting worse, and getting in the way of daily life. In people under 65, early changes can show up in planning, language, behaviour or vision before memory. It also helps to keep the scale in mind: among people in their early 60s, mild cognitive impairment (about 7 in 100) is far more common than dementia from any cause (under 1 in 1,000), and it has many causes besides Alzheimer’s (Petersen 2018) (Hendriks 2021).

What the evaluation involves

  • A history from your parent and from someone who knows them well, plus an exam.
  • Short thinking tests such as the Mini-Cog or MoCA.
  • Blood tests, including thyroid, B12 and a metabolic panel.
  • A brain MRI or CT scan.
  • If the cause is still unclear: specialist tests such as a PET scan, spinal fluid, or one of the new blood tests.

Guideline The 2025 US evaluation guideline (Atri 2025).

Bring a written list of changes with dates and examples, the family history, and every medicine and supplement. That includes over-the-counter sleep aids: diphenhydramine and doxylamine are strongly anticholinergic and can cloud thinking (AGS Beers 2023). Bring glasses and hearing aids too, since poor hearing lowers scores on spoken tests.

If your parent is under 65, Medicare usually doesn’t apply yet, so the evaluation runs through private insurance, Medicaid or self-pay (Medicare.gov). Community health centers charge on a sliding scale (HRSA). Early-onset Alzheimer’s is on Social Security’s fast-track Compassionate Allowances list (SSA).

Raising it gently

Resistance is normal, and often it isn’t stubbornness. Reduced awareness of one’s own changes (anosognosia) is part of the illness itself: studies find it in roughly 20% to 70% of people with Alzheimer’s, depending on how it’s measured (Castrillo Sanz 2016). Fear, stigma and “it’s just age” are the other common barriers (Parker 2020). No trial shows which approach works best, so what follows is expert advice. Expert guidance

  • Write down specific changes first, and think about other causes: stress, depression, a new medicine.
  • Choose one person to talk, ideally one-on-one so it doesn’t feel like an ambush, and pick a calm moment, not the moment of a lapse.
  • Try: “I’ve noticed [a specific change], and I’m concerned. Have you noticed it?”
  • Suggest a check-up and offer to go with them: “There are lots of things that could be causing this.” A general physical or a hearing check is a fair way in.
  • You can write to their doctor beforehand. Privacy rules may stop the doctor from sharing back, but they can read your concerns (Alzheimer’s Association blog).
  • Let the person they trust most raise it, and expect several conversations (10 Steps).

The Alzheimer’s Association helpline is free and open 24/7 at 1-800-272-3900, with interpreters in more than 200 languages (alz.org).

09The viral stories: what holds up

If you’ve seen videos about a man who “should” have got Alzheimer’s decades ago and didn’t, here is what’s real.

Doug Whitney is real

Doug Whitney carries a PSEN2 mutation that gave most of his mother’s siblings early Alzheimer’s. At 71, 18 years past the average onset age for his particular mutation, he still scored normally on thinking tests, though with mild slowing on speed tests. His brain is full of amyloid, but the tau protein that tracks with symptoms stayed confined to one small visual area. His immune and inflammation markers looked like those of people without the mutation, his spinal fluid was rich in “heat shock” proteins, and he carries several gene variants that might protect him (Llibre-Guerra 2025).

The paper never mentions saunas. The authors only “speculate” that heat shock proteins could be involved, and warn that their study of one person “requires cautious interpretation” (Llibre-Guerra 2025). Weak · one person

Where the sauna idea came from

Whitney spent years working in hot ship engine rooms in the Navy. In March 2026 a commentary proposed lifelong heat exposure as a hypothesis, with no new data (Canet 2026). By May, headlines said he was “saved by accidental heat therapy” (New Scientist), and other coverage and wellness posts paired him with a “65%” lower risk from saunas (Earth.com).

Claim by claim

  • “Heat saved him.”UnprovenA hypothesis. Several explanations fit, including his gene variants, and a study of one person can’t separate them. Even in mice, heat lowered tau changes without a heat-shock response (Guisle 2022).
  • “So saunas will protect you too.”StretchHours a day in an engine room for years isn’t a recreational sauna, and the paper doesn’t mention saunas.
  • “Saunas cut Alzheimer’s risk by 65%.”OverstatedFrom one study of 2,315 Finnish men. The 4–7 times a week group was about 200 men with 8 dementia cases, and 2–3 times a week showed no clear difference (Laukkanen 2017). A larger study of 13,994 Finnish men and women found about 19% lower risk and said “no firm conclusions can be made” (Knekt 2020). There is no randomized trial.
  • “He’s immune. It’s a cure.”FalseHe’s free of symptoms so far, with high amyloid. No treatment has come from his case yet.
  • “A brain can carry lots of amyloid without dementia if tau doesn’t spread.”Holds upThe same pattern appears in two protected people in a Colombian family with inherited Alzheimer’s (Arboleda-Velasquez 2019) (Lopera 2023), and 27 carriers of one protective variant developed symptoms about 5 years later than others (Quiroz 2024). That makes tau spread a drug target, not a lifestyle tip.

Whitney’s mutation belongs to the 1%-or-less inherited world. Most family histories are the common late-onset kind, so his case says little about yours.

Saunas are low-risk for most healthy adults and may be good for the heart. Check with a doctor if you have heart disease or uncontrolled blood pressure, skip alcohol, and keep sessions to 15–20 minutes (Harvard Health). Enjoy them if you like them; just don’t let them replace the things with better evidence in section 02.

Other popular claims

  • “Brain-training apps prevent dementia.”Not supportedLumosity paid $2 million in 2016 to settle FTC charges over claims like this (FTC 2016). Gains from brain games mostly stay inside the games (Simons 2016). Play them if you enjoy them.
  • “This supplement protects your memory.”Not supportedGinkgo didn’t reduce dementia in a 3,069-person trial (DeKosky 2008). Fish-oil pills (Kang 2022) and B-vitamin complexes (Clarke 2014) didn’t help thinking in trials, and high-dose vitamin E was linked to higher death rates (Miller 2005). A court barred Prevagen’s memory claims (FTC 2024), “nootropic” stacks have been found to contain unapproved drugs (Cohen 2021), and lithium orotate has only mouse data (Aron 2025). The WHO’s 2026 guideline recommends against B, E, omega-3 and multivitamin supplements for dementia risk unless you have a diagnosed deficiency (WHO 2026). Eating fish is a different matter (section 02).
  • “A daily multivitamin keeps your brain young.”Small, 60+ onlyIn people over 60 it nudged memory-test scores up slightly (Vyas 2024). There’s no dementia data, and nothing in younger adults.
  • “This program reverses Alzheimer’s.”Not shownThe Bredesen/ReCODE claims rest on an uncontrolled pilot of 25 people (Toups 2022) and an unpublished trial, run by people with a commercial interest.
  • “Ozempic-type drugs protect the brain.”Not for healthy peopleSemaglutide didn’t slow early Alzheimer’s in two large trials (Cummings 2026). Hints of prevention come only from trials in people with diabetes (Seminer 2025).
  • “Hormone therapy prevents Alzheimer’s.”Not supportedTrials found it neutral when started around menopause and harmful when started at 65 or later (section 02). The “35% lower risk” in a 2025 FDA press release (FDA 2025) traces to a 1996 case–control study, not to trials (Paganini-Hill 1996).
  • “A glass of wine is good for the brain.”Not supportedNon-drinkers look worse in some studies because they include former heavy drinkers and people who are ill. Genetic studies find no protective amount (Topiwala 2025).

10Join the research

No drug prevents Alzheimer’s yet, but there is something useful you can do today: sign up to help find one.

Registries you can join now (18 and over, free)

  • Alzheimer’s Prevention Registry
    Run by Banner Alzheimer’s Institute. Emails you about studies near you or online.
  • UCSF Brain Health Registry
    Online questionnaires and brain games about every six months, under three hours a year.
  • Alzheimer’s Association TrialMatch
    Matches you to studies that are recruiting now, including ones for healthy volunteers.
  • Alzheimers.gov Clinical Trials Finder
    The National Institute on Aging’s search by location, with email alerts.
  • Your nearest Alzheimer’s Disease Research Center
    Many recruit healthy volunteers and family members; age limits vary by center.

Some well-known programs start later: GeneMatch takes people from 50 (NCT02564692), and the APT Webstudy from 50 to 85 (Alzheimers.gov). Families with a confirmed inherited mutation have their own study, DIAN, which takes adult children from 18 (DIAN).

Where prevention drugs stand

No drug is approved to prevent Alzheimer’s at any age. Earlier prevention trials failed: solanezumab in healthy older people with amyloid (Sperling 2023), crenezumab in a Colombian family with inherited Alzheimer’s (NeurologyLive 2022), and a BACE-inhibitor pill that was stopped because it made thinking slightly worse (Novartis 2019). The one encouraging signal is small: among 22 people with inherited mutations treated longest with gantenerumab, the risk of symptoms appeared roughly halved, without a randomized comparison group (Bateman 2025). Weak · small, open-label

Large prevention trials of donanemab, lecanemab and newer antibodies are running in people aged 55 to 80 with early brain changes. The first is due to finish in November 2027 and the next in December 2028 (TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 3) (AHEAD 3-45). The only drug-prevention trial open to younger adults is for families with a known inherited mutation (DIAN-TU).
