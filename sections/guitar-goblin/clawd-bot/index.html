How the legs work

Each leg is one rigid 16 mm square block, like Clawd's stubby legs, poking out through a slot in the underside of the shell. Two servos per leg, both inside the shell, give the foot its two motions:

  1. Two 3 mm steel guide rods per leg, fixed between the base deck and the top deck.
  2. The swing servo rides on a printed carriage that slides on the rods (LM3UU bushings). The leg hangs from its horn, so turning it swings the foot forward and back.
  3. The lift servo is fixed to the frame. Its horn carries a pin…
  4. …that sits in a horizontal slot in the carriage: a Scotch yoke. As the horn turns, the pin slides along the slot and lifts the carriage: carriage height = 61 + 16·sin(lift angle) mm.
  5. The leg, a rigid 16 × 16 mm printed block, 56 mm from the swing axis to the floor. It ends in a rounded toe (4 mm radius), because a flat sole tilted at full swing would dig its corner into the floor.
  6. The slot in the underside of the shell, 20 × 44 mm, gives the leg room to swing ±23° and to rise 18 mm into the body.

The lift does two jobs. In the air it raises the foot 14 mm (trot) to 12 mm (creep). On the ground it cancels the arc a swinging leg would trace: as the leg tilts, the carriage drops just enough (56·(1 − cos swing) mm, up to 4.5 mm) that the foot slides back along the floor in a dead straight line. So walking straight, planted feet stay exactly where they landed.

Other ways to do it: a two-axis gimbal at the top of the leg (fore/aft plus a sideways roll to lift) only lifts a short leg a few millimetres, and would splay the feet. A four-bar linkage per leg also works, but it's more links and pivots to print and tune, and its foot path is curved. The yoke is compact, and the up/down is clean. Version 1 of this page had a coxa and knee servo sticking out of each corner; this one keeps all eight inside.

Test rig: print one leg first

Before printing four, build one leg module on a bench stand. It's exactly the robot's module: the lift servo and its crank, the Scotch yoke, the carriage on two rods, the swing servo and the leg, between two rod blocks at the robot's deck heights. A floor plate lets the foot touch down, and a 3 mm plate at the shell's underside height has the real 20 × 44 mm leg slot, so you can check the clearance too. A leg that works here drops straight into the robot.

Assembly tap a step to see it

  1. Frame. Bolt the column to the base with three M3 × 12 screws and nuts. Screw the base to the bench (four wood screws) or clamp its edge.
  2. Lift servo. Drop it into the column's servo plate from the outside, shaft outward and at the top end, and fix the tabs with two M2 self-tappers.
  3. Carriage. Press an LM3UU bushing into each housing from the top, until it sits on the lip. Fit the swing servo into the carriage pocket the same way, shaft outward at the bottom end.
  4. Rods. Hold the carriage between the rod blocks and push each 3 mm × 65 mm rod down through the top block, the bushing and into the bottom block. A drop of CA glue in the blocks if they're loose. The carriage should drop under its own weight.
  5. Leg. Run the test sketch's h (both servos at 90°). Screw the swing servo's single-arm horn into the leg's pocket, then push it onto the spline with the leg straight down, and fit the horn screw.
  6. Crank and yoke. Screw the lift servo's single-arm horn into the crank's pocket (trim the arm to 14 mm if it's longer). With the servo still at 90°, push it onto the spline pointing at the carriage, level. Turn an M2 × 10 screw through the crank's outer hole so it slides into the carriage's slot.

Hardware for one leg

  • 2 × MG90S servos (with their horns and screws), 2 × 3 mm steel rod cut to 65 mm, 2 × LM3UU bushings.
  • 1 × M2 × 10 screw (the yoke pin), 4 × M2 × 6–8 self-tappers (servo tabs), 3 × M3 × 12 + nuts, 4 wood screws or a clamp.
  • To drive it: an ESP32 dev board and a PCA9685 (the robot's wiring, channels 0 and 1), or straight from ESP32 pins 18 and 19 with the ESP32Servo library. A 5–6 V supply good for 2 A or more on the servo rail, with a 470–1000 µF capacitor, and a common ground with the ESP32.

What to check

  • The carriage slides freely with the servos unplugged: lift it and it drops on its own, from the bottom of its travel to the top, with no tight spot.
  • No binding at the ends: on the l sweep the pin runs the whole slot without touching its ends, and the servo doesn't buzz at either end.
  • Foot lift at least 12 mm at the top of a step in c and t: 12 mm in creep, 14 mm in trot.
  • Stance is flat: during the sliding part of a step the toe stays on the floor and moves 30 mm in a straight line. If it skips or digs in, adjust the lift trim.
  • Clearance: the leg never touches the slot plate, and the crank never touches the leg.
  • Copy your trims into the robot sketch's TRIM array for that leg (channels 2 and 3 for a front-right leg).

What was checked before publishing

  • Every printed STL is a closed, consistently wound volume (trimesh: watertight, winding and volume checks; admesh: one part, 0 degenerate facets, 0 edges fixed, 0 backwards edges), sits on the bed and fits 220 × 220.
  • No two parts intersect anywhere in the range: frame plus lift servo plus rods, carriage plus swing servo plus bushings, leg, and crank plus pin, every pair, at 81 lift and swing combinations from −37° to +34° lift and ±24° swing (boolean intersections with manifold3d). The exception is the leg against the slot plate. The slot limits swing to ±26° with the carriage at the bottom and ±16° at the top, and all 324 poses the gaits and the test sweeps use are inside that.
  • The toe stays within 0.5 mm of the floor in all of those poses. It's rounded (4 mm radius) because a flat 16 mm sole tilted 23° would put its corner 3 mm into the floor.
  • Both sketches compile with g++ -Wall -Wextra against stand-in Arduino headers, and the robot sketch's servo pulses match the sim's to within 1 µs.

Size and shape

  • Shell 150 × 124 × 84 mm (wide, deep, tall), printed in coral, with the eyes and claws as separate pieces. That's bigger than a bare minimum so four leg modules, the battery and two boards fit inside. Its underside sits 28 mm off the floor.
  • Legs: 16 mm square, 56 mm from the swing axis to the toe, at the corners of a 110 × 72 mm rectangle, directly under the shell.
  • Inside: four leg modules in the corners (swing servo on its carriage, lift servo beside it), the LiPo flat in the middle of the base deck, and the ESP32, PCA9685 and both bucks on a top deck held up by the guide rods. The module is the one on the test rig, part for part.
  • Step: 30 mm stride, 12–14 mm foot lift. About 17 mm/s in creep and 75 mm/s in trot at 1×.
  • Mass about 480 g: servos 107 g, LiPo 75 g, boards 30 g, rods and bushings 37 g, printed parts 200 g, wire and screws 30 g.

Servo angle = 90 + Dir × joint angle. The four modules are mirror images (left/right, and the lift servo sits on the inboard side of front and hind modules), so their servos turn opposite ways for the same move. If a joint goes the wrong way on your build, flip its Dir in the sketch.

Power, and not browning out

  • Servo rail at 6.0 V (MG90S are rated 4.8–6 V; 6 V gives the most torque, never go above). Each servo pulls 150–250 mA moving and close to 1 A stalled. The four lift servos hold the robot up even when it stands still, so expect 0.3–0.8 A just standing, 1.5–2.5 A walking, and 3–4 A peaks. Use a 5 A buck.
  • The ESP32 gets its own 5 V buck. If it shares the servo rail, a few servos starting at once sag the rail and the ESP32 resets ("Brownout detector was triggered" in the serial monitor).
  • 1000 µF across V+ and GND at the PCA9685 soaks up the start-up spikes. Mind the polarity.
  • Soft start. The sketch wakes the servos one at a time, 150 ms apart, lift servos first so the body comes up before the legs line up.
  • One common ground for everything, or the I²C signals get flaky.
  • Battery. A 1300 mAh 2S pack is about 9.6 Wh: roughly 40 minutes of walking. Charge it out of the robot on a balance charger.

Gaits and the joint-angle tables

Both gaits are loops of keyframes. A foot on the ground slides back 30 mm in a straight line (that pushes the body forward), then lifts, swings forward and comes down. Creep also sways the body 12 mm forward before each hind step and back before each front step (all the planted feet slide together), so the centre of mass is never near the edge of the three feet holding it up.

The angles come from inverse kinematics: swing = asin(forward / 56), then the carriage goes to foot height + 56·cos(swing), and lift = asin((carriage − 61) / 16). Values are joint angles in degrees: swing + = foot forward, lift + = carriage up. Shaded lift cells are steps, with the foot in the air. Steering shortens the inside legs' strides (skid steering, like a tank) and re-solves the lift so the foot stays on the floor. The playing row lights up as the sim runs.

Foot scuff (the number in the sim): walking straight it's under 1 mm, just the tables' whole-degree rounding. Turning is different: the legs only move fore and aft, so the robot turns by taking longer steps on one side, and the feet scrub sideways up to about 8 mm a step at full turn. Rubber or TPU soles and a smooth floor help.

Build notes

  • Centre the servos before the horns go on. Run the sketch without sending w: it parks every servo at the first creep keyframe. Fit each horn as close as the spline allows, then fix the last few degrees in TRIM until all four soles touch the floor together.
  • Torque check. In trot two feet carry 480 g, about 2.4 N each. The lift servo holds that through a 16 mm crank: 0.038 N·m (0.39 kg·cm), about 5× margin on an MG90S. The swing servo sees the floor push 22 mm ahead of its axis at full swing: 0.052 N·m (0.53 kg·cm), about 3.5× margin. The rods take the sideways loads, so the servo shafts only carry turning.
  • Slot clearance. The 16 mm leg gets 2 mm each side across the 20 mm slot. Along it, the slot allows ±26° of swing with the carriage at the bottom and ±16° at the top. Every pose the gaits use is inside that (checked on the test rig's meshes), so the shell never touches the legs.
  • Balance. With the sway, the centre of mass stays at least 8 mm inside the triangle of planted feet in creep (about 2 mm at full turn): that's the "balance margin" in the sim. Keep the LiPo centred and low, as drawn. Trot has only two feet down at a time and stays up by being quick.
  • Print the frame, carriages and legs in PLA at 3 walls and 30% infill, and the shell at 2 walls. Ream the yoke slot so the pin slides without play. Print the eyes in black and press-fit them. The claws hang on 1.2 mm TPU flexures, so they jiggle as it walks: no servos.
  • Next steps: a phone remote over Bluetooth, or an IMU so it can feel when it's tipping.
