  • Tokyo → Hakone

    Romancecar: about 80 minutes, no changeShinkansen: about 35 + 15 minutes, one change

    Three realistic ways, and the choice is mostly about which side of Tokyo you are staying on.

    • Romancecar from Shinjuku. Odakyu’s limited express runs direct to Hakone-Yumoto with no change. Odakyu gives about 80 minutes; on its printed timetable the trains take 75 to 107. Every seat is reserved, so buy the seat ticket ahead. On that timetable there are 16 direct trains on weekdays and 24 at weekends, the first at 7:37 and 7:00.
    • Shinkansen from Tokyo Station or Shinagawa to Odawara, then the Hakone Tozan Line to Hakone-Yumoto. About 35 minutes and about 15, with one change; JNTO gives about 55 minutes in all. Only the Kodama (two an hour) and one Hikari every two hours stop at Odawara. No Nozomi does. At Odawara the Shinkansen gate is on the first floor and the gate for Hakone on the third.
    • Ordinary JR train to Odawara: about 70 to 90 minutes from Tokyo, Shinagawa or Shinjuku, then the same 15-minute change. No reservation.
    • What it costs in changes: none from Shinjuku by Romancecar; one at Odawara any other way. If you are staying near Tokyo Station or Shinagawa, the Shinkansen is quicker door to door. If you are near Shinjuku, the Romancecar is one seat all the way.

  • Hakone in one night: the loop

    about 2¼ to 3 hours of riding, before any stopsthe operator’s own day: 9:20 to 17:00 with three stops

    The classic circuit uses five kinds of transport, all run by the Odakyu Hakone group, and is usually done counter-clockwise from Hakone-Yumoto. japan-guide says it can be done as a day trip from Tokyo but recommends two days and a night, which is what your plan gives it.

    • 1. Mountain train, Hakone-Yumoto to Gora: about 40 minutes, every 15 to 20, up three switchbacks. 2. Cable car, Gora to Sounzan: about 10 minutes. 3. Ropeway, Sounzan over Owakudani to Togendai: 30 to 45 minutes (the operator’s own pages disagree), with a change of gondola at Owakudani. 4. Sightseeing ship across Lake Ashi to Moto-Hakone or Hakone-machi: 25 to 40 minutes. 5. Bus back to Hakone-Yumoto: 25 to 40 minutes depending on the route.
    • No operator states a total. Added up, the rides come to between about 2 h 10 and 2 h 55, leaving out every wait, change and stop. The sum is ours.
    • What limits the day: the ropeway takes its last passengers at 17:00 (16:15 in December and January), and on the ship timetable valid to 30 November the last sailing from Togendai that calls at Moto-Hakone leaves at 16:25. By our arithmetic, someone starting the loop at Hakone-Yumoto after about 14:15 cannot finish it that day even without stopping.
    • What is realistic in one night: split it. Do the mountain half on the day you arrive and the lake half the next morning, or the other way round if your ryokan is by the lake. The operator’s own model day for the whole loop with three stops starts at Hakone-Yumoto at 9:20 and gets back at 17:00, which from Tokyo means a train before 8:00.
    • Where you sleep changes the split. Hakone-Yumoto is the start and end of the loop and the easiest base; Gora is the top of the mountain railway; the Lake Ashi side is the far end. Hakone’s tourism site says all areas are connected.
    • The Hakone Freepass is a two-day or three-day pass covering every leg of the loop, and the replacement buses during planned ropeway maintenance. It is sold from Shinjuku, with one Odakyu return trip, and from Odawara without. The Romancecar seat is extra. We have not worked out whether it pays for itself.
    • Owakudani: Kanagawa Prefecture asks people with asthma or bronchial, respiratory or heart conditions not to visit, because of the volcanic gas.

  • Luggage: send it or carry it

    Tokyo to a Kyoto hotel: next day at the earliest

    Hakone is the awkward stop for suitcases: large luggage is not allowed on the ropeway at all, and the mountain train and the buses are small. There are two ways round it.

    • Send the big cases from Tokyo straight to Kyoto and take an overnight bag to Hakone. Yamato Transport forwards bags hotel to hotel with no reservation; its own example is Tokyo to a Kyoto hotel, delivered “as early as the next day”. Bags handed over on the morning you leave Tokyo are therefore due the day you reach Kyoto. Sending them a day earlier removes the risk of arriving before they do. The Kyoto hotel has to agree to receive them: ask first.
    • Or bring them, and use the luggage service at Hakone-Yumoto Station. Bags dropped at the station counter between 8:30 and 12:00 are at the hotel from 15:00. On the way out, bags given to the hotel by 10:00 can be collected at the station from 13:30 to 18:30. It delivers only to partner hotels, so check that yours is one.
    • That pick-up window matters: with the return service you cannot leave Hakone-Yumoto with your bags before 13:30, which rules out the midday Hikari from Odawara. The inference is ours.
    • Hakone-Yumoto Station has coin lockers and staffed storage. Odawara Station has staffed storage at the Odakyu service centre; lockers there we could not confirm.
    • On the Shinkansen, a bag whose three sides add up to more than 160 cm needs a reserved seat with an oversized-baggage area. Those seats are in reserved cars only.

  • Hakone → Kyoto, via Odawara

    about 15 minutes to Odawaraabout 2 h 05 on a through Hikariabout 2 h 40 to 3 h with a change at Nagoya

    Hakone-Yumoto to Odawara on the Hakone Tozan Line, then the Tokaido Shinkansen west. Which Shinkansen is the whole question, because most of them do not stop at Odawara.

    • No Nozomi stops at Odawara. Every Kodama does, two an hour. Of the Hikari, one every two hours does: on the timetable columns we read it leaves Odawara at 8:11, 10:11, 12:07 and 14:07 and runs through to Kyoto in about 2 h 05 with no change.
    • If no Hikari is due: a Kodama to Nagoya and a Nozomi on from there, about 2 h 40 to 3 h in all depending on the connection. The Kodama that goes all the way takes about three hours. This is our reading of the timetable; no source describes it in words.
    • japan-guide’s wording is “one direct Hikari train every 2-5 hours”, so the two-hour pattern may not hold all day. We read the morning and early-afternoon columns only. Look up your own train.
    • Hikari and Kodama have non-reserved cars. For a Japan Rail Pass holder the through Hikari is covered and a Nozomi from Nagoya is not.
    • From the Lake Ashi side there is another way out: an hourly bus from Moto-Hakone to Mishima Station, about 50 minutes, where a Hikari to Kyoto also stops every two hours.
    • Door to door, japan-guide puts Hakone to Kyoto at about three and a half hours.

Day trips All optional. None is in your plan; each is here with what it costs in travel.

  • From Tokyo: Kamakura, Nikko, Yokohama

    Kamakura: 55 minutes each wayNikko: about 2 hours each wayYokohama: 15 to 30 minutes

    Day 5 is the day for one of these, if you want one. Your plan already has a day in the mountains at Hakone, so none is needed.

    • Kamakura. The JR Yokosuka Line runs direct from Tokyo Station in 55 minutes.   • From Kyoto: Nara

    about 45 minutes by JRabout 35 minutes by Kintetsu limited express

    The obvious one, and the cheapest in time. It fits Day 10 because Fushimi Inari is on the same line.

    • JR: the Miyakoji Rapid on the JR Nara Line, about 45 minutes, every 30 minutes, no reservation. Kyoto, Tofukuji and Inari are consecutive stops on this line, so Nara can follow Fushimi Inari. Check on the day which trains stop at Inari.
    • Kintetsu: the limited express from Kintetsu Kyoto Station takes about 35 minutes, twice an hour, every seat reserved with a supplement ticket; the hourly express takes about 45.
    • Kintetsu-Nara is the nearer station to the park: about five minutes on foot, against about twenty from JR Nara.

  • From Kyoto: Osaka

    about 30 minutes by JR special rapidabout 15 minutes by Shinkansen, to Shin-Osaka only

    Close enough to be an evening rather than a day: go for dinner in Dotonbori and come back.

By season We do not know when you are going, so nothing above assumes a month. This is what changes.

  • Late March to early April cherry blossom

    In a normal year the blossom opens in Tokyo on 24 March and is full on 31 March; in Kyoto, 26 March and 4 April. It moves by a week or more from year to year. This is the most crowded time: Kyoto says its buses are at their worst, and Shinjuku Gyoen may require reservations at weekends.

  • Late April to early May Golden Week

    In 2027, 29 April is a holiday and 1 to 5 May is a five-day run. Trains, sights and hotels are very crowded and rooms book out well ahead. If you can choose, do not choose this week.

  • June to mid-July rainy season

    On the weather agency’s normals the rains start around 6 or 7 June and end around 19 July in both Tokyo and Kyoto. It does not rain every day, and June is a quiet month. Mount Fuji is at its most hidden: in 2025 the whole mountain was visible at 8 a.m. on 2 days in June.

  • July to September heat and typhoons

    The normal August daily high is 31.3 °C in Tokyo and 33.7 °C in Kyoto. Typhoons peak in August and September. Obon, around 13 to 16 August, is one of the three busiest travel periods. Kyoto’s Gion Matsuri fills July, with processions on the 17th and 24th.

  • November to early December autumn leaves

    japan-guide’s chart for an average year: Hakone from early to late November, Kyoto from mid-November to early December, Tokyo from late November to early December. Kyoto’s temples open at night on dates that change each year, and its buses are again at their worst. In November 2025 the whole of Fuji was visible on 19 mornings.

  • December to February cold, clear, short days

    Tokyo’s sunniest and driest months, and the best odds for Fuji: the whole mountain was visible at 8 a.m. on 19 to 22 mornings a month in January, February and December 2025. Kyoto is cold and dry but not sunny. Days end early: the Hakone ropeway stops at 16:15 in December and January, Meiji Jingu shuts at about 16:00 in December, and the Sagano scenic train does not run from 30 December to the end of February. Many museums, markets and restaurants close for some days between 29 December and 4 January.

The Fuji figures are Fuji City’s daily record for 2025, taken from the south side of the mountain, not from Hakone: they show the season, not what you will see.   • Hakone or Kawaguchiko, for Mount Fuji

    A friend suggested a night at Lake Kawaguchiko for a closer look at Fuji. Your plan already has a Fuji-area night, in Hakone, so this is a comparison and not a change.

    • What each gives. Kawaguchiko is one of the Fuji Five Lakes, at the mountain’s northern foot, and the mountain is the point of going. Hakone is a hot-spring resort where Fuji is seen from particular spots: the ropeway, the lake, the shore at Moto-Hakone. Hakone’s own tourism body, which is not neutral, puts it as “some amazing views” at Kawaguchiko against “a more balanced experience” in Hakone.
    • Getting there. Tokyo to Kawaguchiko is under two hours: a direct train from Shinjuku (fastest 1 h 53, four round trips a day) or a highway bus (about 1 h 45).
    • Getting on to Kyoto is where it costs: about three and a half hours from Kawaguchiko, by a 90-minute bus to Mishima and then a Hikari, against about two hours twenty from Hakone-Yumoto.
    • Either way it is the weather’s decision. No record exists for either lake; the one official count, from the south side, has the whole mountain visible on 61 to 79 mornings in a hundred from November to February and 7 to 16 from June to August.
